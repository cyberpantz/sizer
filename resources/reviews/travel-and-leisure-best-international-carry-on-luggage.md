## Testing methodology (T+L's description)

1. Pack each bag with clothing, shoes, a toiletry bag, and other travel necessities.
2. Roll wheeled bags around cones and over **gravel, carpet, and stone** to judge wheel smoothness and maneuverability.
3. Durability: drop off a table, then strike several times with a baseball bat.
4. Fit: lift into real overhead bins and slide under real airplane seats on their airplane set.
5. Long-term: editors travel with each bag for a further six months.

Evaluation axes stated: **capacity, design, maneuverability, durability**, with a hard filter on the 22-inch max height.

## International sizing and weight reference (from the FAQ)

- **International vs. domestic:** international carry-ons generally must be smaller than the US-standard 22 × 14 × 9 in — sometimes **under 21.7 in tall including handle and wheels** — with stricter weight rules. Limits vary by carrier and even by route. Their rule of thumb: when in doubt, take the more compact bag.
- **Weight limits:** many international carriers cap carry-ons at **15–22 lb**; British Airways, KLM, and Air France are cited as less strict. A portable luggage scale is recommended. (This is why every pick lists an empty weight — a 10.5 lb bag leaves only ~5 lb of packing allowance on a 15 lb carrier.)
- **Allowance:** generally **one carry-on plus one personal item** on international flights; personal item = small backpack, purse, or tote that fits under the seat.
- **Warranties:** vary by brand; usually cover manufacturing defects only, not misuse, cosmetic damage (scuffs, dents, stains), or baggage-handler damage. Often **valid only on direct purchases**, not third-party resellers, and require proof of purchase. Advice: test wheels, zippers, and features at home before the trip.

## Buying guidance specific to this article

- **Match the bag to the trip:** bulky gear (ski, camping, cold-weather coats and boots) argues for a larger or expandable carry-on; backpacking argues for a backpack or wheeled duffel.
- **Organization matters more here than in domestic guides** — because international carry-ons are smaller, the author ranks organizational features (compression straps, exterior laptop pockets, laundry bags, packing cubes) as the number-one purchase criterion.
