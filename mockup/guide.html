Why bags get stopped at the gate

Almost none of it is because people didn't read the rules. Here's what actually goes wrong, and what to do about each one.

Written 28 August 2026 · updated when the rules change

1. Your bag is probably bigger than the label says

This is the single most common reason a compliant-looking bag gets turned away, and almost no guide mentions it.

Consumer Reports measured a Briggs & Riley sold as 22 × 14 × 9 in and found it was actually 22.5 × 14.5 × 9.25 — over the limit at American, Delta and United, straight out of the box. They measured an Eagle Creek Tarmac 22, also sold as 22 × 14 × 9, at 22.25 × 15 × 8.5.

Source: Consumer Reports luggage testing, via syndicated report

The reason is mundane rather than sinister. Most makers measure the case — the box you pack things into. A Samsonite spokesperson has confirmed the company does not include handles or wheels. Airlines measure whatever sticks out. Those are two different numbers for the same object, and only one of them is used at the gate.

And the gap isn't consistent, which is why you can't correct for it. The Briggs & Riley ran over on all three sides. The Eagle Creek ran over on width but under on depth. Add a centimetre to every number on the label and you'll still be caught — just on a different side.

Measuring is the only thing that works. It takes two minutes and we've written down exactly how in section 3.

2. Three bags, three places — and they're not the same thing

Most bad outcomes start with treating "carry-on" as one category. It's three, and the difference is about where the bag physically goes.

Typical limits. Exact numbers vary by airline and by ticket — the point is the relative size.

On the cheapest tickets from several airlines, the green box is all you get. Not a smaller suitcase — no suitcase. That catches more people than any size limit does, and it's covered in section 5.

3. How to measure yours

Two minutes, a tape measure, and no special knowledge. This is exactly the method we use on every bag we publish.

  1. Empty the bag and stand it upright on a hard floor, against a wall.
  2. Push the telescoping handle all the way down. An extended handle isn't how it's measured, and leaving it up will scare you unnecessarily.
  3. Measure the widest point on each side — not the flat face. Include the wheels, the feet, the handle housing and any side pocket that sits proud when the bag is packed.
  4. Write it down to the nearest half centimetre. Round up, never down.
  5. Compare against the airline, not against the label. The label is the manufacturer's number and it isn't the one that gets used.
If your bag matches its label, that's genuinely worth knowing. Some do. It means the maker measured the way an airline does, and it's a reasonable thing to look for next time you buy.

4. Who actually checks — and who doesn't

Knowing a limit isn't the same as knowing whether anyone enforces it. These are the facts we score airlines on.

Some airlines pay staff to find oversize bags. Frontier has paid gate agents $10 per bag caught at the gate and Spirit $5 — together around $26 million in such bonuses across 2022 and 2023. Ryanair raised its own spotting bonus from €1.50 to €2.50 per bag and removed the monthly cap.

Source: company disclosures and contemporaneous reporting · see our methodology

And some have moved the other way. American removed the measuring frames from every gate in October 2025 and told staff to give passengers the benefit of the doubt. So a bag a centimetre over is a very different proposition at American than at Wizz Air, even though the published limits are similar.

US enforcement is splitting in two. Budget carriers are tightening while the big legacy airlines loosen at the gate. That's a real change, it happened recently, and most published guidance hasn't caught up with it.

There's a third case worth knowing: regional jets. On a CRJ-200 or an ERJ-145, the overhead lockers are smaller than the airline's own published limit. Your bag can be entirely legal and still be taken at the aircraft door. It's handed back at the door on arrival, and it costs nothing — but if you were relying on having it with you, plan differently.

5. The ticket trap

The same words mean different things at different airlines. This is the biggest single cause of a wrong answer.

On the cheapest ticketOverhead locker?
Ryanair · Wizz Air · VuelingNo — under-seat bag only, at any size
United (domestic Basic Economy)No — personal item only
American · Delta (Basic Economy)Yes — a full carry-on is included
British Airways (hand baggage only)Yes, and a generous one
Read that table again. "Basic Economy" gets you a carry-on at American and Delta, and nothing but a personal item at United. Same phrase, opposite outcome, same country. Nobody could reasonably be expected to know this — which is why we ask which ticket you bought rather than assuming.

6. Weight versus pieces

Two different systems for checked bags, and mixing them up on a connecting trip is expensive.

SystemHow it worksWhere
PieceA set number of bags, each with its own weight limit — typically 23 kgTransatlantic, transpacific, the Americas
WeightA total allowance you can split across as many bags as you likeMuch of Asia, many intra-Europe routes

The trap is the connection. If one trip has both systems on it, the rules can change between legs — and the strictest one is what you have to pack for.

Weight catches Americans out in another way: US airlines almost never weigh a carry-on, and much of Asia does it as routine. Singapore and Cathay Pacific allow 7 kg in the cabin. A hard-shell suitcase can use half of that empty, before you put anything in it.

7. If you're already over

Usually fixable, and rarely by buying anything.

SituationWhat to do
Slightly over, relaxed airlinePack it thin, don't overstuff outer pockets, and don't use the expansion zip
Slightly over, strict airlineBuy the hold bag online before you go — it's typically two to three times the price at the airport
Only the cabin size is the problemCheck whether a fare upgrade costs less than the hold bag. On Ryanair it often does.
Soft-sided bagIt compresses. A soft bag will often go into a frame that a hard shell won't.
Regional jet legNothing to do. It's taken at the door and returned at the door, free.
One booking or several? On a single ticket, a checked bag goes to your final destination — you can't check it for one leg and carry it on the next. Booked separately, you can.
