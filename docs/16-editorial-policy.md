# Editorial policy

How we write about named companies. Short, and non-negotiable — this is what separates a measurement site from a grievance site.

## The stance

> **We publish measurements. We do not publish accusations.**

Every page names real brands and real airlines, with numbers that sometimes make them look bad. That's fine and it's the point. What it must never become is a site whose apparent purpose is catching companies out.

Two reasons, and the second matters more:

1. **Legal exposure.** A dated, sourced measurement is reporting. "Away's bag is refused by 20 of 24 airlines" is a characterisation, and characterisations are what draw letters.
2. **Persuasion.** If every page reads as an attack, a reader discounts the numbers as motivated. Flat presentation lands harder. **The table is damning enough without help.**

## Rules

### Describe the mechanism, never the motive

| ✅ Publish | ❌ Never |
|---|---|
| "We measured it 1 cm over the label on two sides." | "Away inflates its numbers." |
| "Most makers measure the case; airlines measure whatever sticks out." | "Manufacturers mislead buyers about size." |
| "Accepted by 10 of 24 airlines we track." | "Refused by 14 airlines." |
| "The listing's stated size exceeds this limit." | "Amazon's claim is false." |

The right column may even be *true*. It is still not ours to say, and saying it costs more than it earns.

### Rotate the examples

No brand is the running example for more than one thing at a time. Away was, briefly, the homepage hero *and* the bag-page template — that concentration is what this rule exists to prevent.

- **Homepage scenarios:** seven, each a different company. A CI check asserts no brand repeats.
- **Bag-page template example:** a *mixed* result, not the worst in the catalogue. Currently Osprey Farpoint 40 at 10 of 24 — fine across the US and much of Asia, caught out by European budget carriers. Representative, useful, not a hit piece.
- **Guide and social examples:** rotate deliberately. If a brand has appeared twice in a month, pick another.

### Publish the good news too

When a label is accurate, **say so warmly and prominently**:

> **The label is accurate.** We measured this bag at exactly the size Osprey publishes — including the wheels and handle. That is less common than it should be, and worth knowing.

This is not politeness. A site that only ever reports discrepancies looks like it goes looking for them. A site that also certifies accuracy is measuring. **The vindications are what make the criticisms credible**, and they cost nothing to publish.

### No page ships without its evidence

A bag page may not go live unless it carries:

- The measurement photo
- The date and the measurer
- A link to each airline's own baggage page for every row in the table

If any of those is missing, the page is an opinion about a company. With them, it's a record.

### Language to avoid

`refused` · `rejected` · `lies` · `misleading` · `deceptive` · `false claim` · `inflated` · `gets away with` · `caught`

All are emotive, none are needed. `accepted by`, `over the limit`, `differs from the label`, and `we measured` carry the same information at none of the cost.

## When a brand pushes back

Expected, and it's an opportunity rather than a crisis.

1. **Re-measure.** Publicly, and say we're doing it.
2. **If we were wrong, correct it prominently** — top of the page, in the changelog, dated. A visible correction is worth more trust than never being wrong.
3. **If we were right, publish the photograph** and leave the number alone. No commentary.
4. **Never remove a measurement under pressure** without re-measuring first. The archive and changelog exist so that quiet edits are impossible.

Offer any brand a re-measure on request, and publish the result whichever way it falls. That standing offer, stated on `/methodology`, is cheap and it is most of the defence.
